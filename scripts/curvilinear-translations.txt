48|Average velocity and acceleration vectors
49|Consider the interval from t₁ to t₂, with Δt = t₂ − t₁ > 0. The subscript m denotes the average over this interval.
50|Average velocity vector
51|The average velocity vector is the displacement vector divided by the time taken for that displacement.
52|For nonzero displacement, it points in the direction of displacement, from the initial to the final position. SI unit: m/s.
53|Average acceleration vector
54|The average acceleration vector is the change in the velocity vector divided by the time over which this change occurs.
55|For a nonzero change, it has the direction and orientation of the vector Δ
56|. This describes a change in magnitude, direction or both. SI unit: m/s².
57|For average acceleration, velocities are subtracted
58|as vectors
59|Average speed and the magnitude of average velocity
60|These are two different scalar quantities, both measured in m/s. The first uses the
61|distance travelled along the trajectory
62|, while the second uses the
63|magnitude of the displacement between the initial and final positions
64|. In this lesson, we explicitly call the first quantity “average speed” to avoid confusion.
65|Average speed
66|The total distance travelled divided by the total time. It includes the entire path, including any sections travelled after reversing direction. It has no direction or orientation.
67|Magnitude of average velocity
68|The straight-line distance between the initial and final positions divided by the same time. It depends on the endpoints and duration, not on the length of the path followed.
69|Comparison over the same interval
70|Equality holds for
71|rectilinear motion without a change of direction
72|during the interval. The particle follows a
73|straight trajectory
74|from its initial to its final position without reversing. Speed need not be constant. For a curved path of nonzero length, average speed is strictly greater.

245|A car travels at a constant 60 km/h along a winding road. Does it have acceleration?
246|Its speed is constant (aₜ = 0), but its direction changes in bends, so there is normal acceleration aₙ = v²/R, pointing towards the inside of the curve.
247|What happens to aₙ if speed doubles on the same curve?
248|quadruples
249|: aₙ = v²/R depends on speed squared. At the same radius of curvature, doubling speed makes normal acceleration four times as large.
250|When is total acceleration purely tangential?
251|When the normal component is zero and speed changes. This occurs in nonuniform rectilinear motion and can occur at a point of zero curvature on a curved path.
